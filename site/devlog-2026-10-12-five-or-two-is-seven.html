Five Or Two Is Seven

How a single bitwise OR inside Unreal Engine 5.8 can crash two perfectly good particle effects, and what it taught us about trusting fixes that have not shipped yet.

Published12 October 2026
CategoryEngine & Tooling
SeriesDev Brief

Header image: A lower hall inside one of COGHEIM's undersea structures, lit through a ring of glass overhead. Engine capture, work in progress.

Most bugs that cost a team a week are not exotic. They are one wrong operator in a place nobody thinks to look. This week we read one of those in the engine that runs COGHEIM, and it is a good story about how a game engine combines information, and about how a small studio decides what to trust.

Two good effects, one bad night

COGHEIM is built in Unreal Engine 5.8, and it renders materials through Substrate, the engine's newer material system. Substrate can describe surfaces in layers: a coat of grime over aged iron, a film of water over brass, a lacquer on glass. Richer surfaces cost more memory per pixel, so every Substrate material reports how much space it needs. Think of it as a number of slots. A simple material might need two. A layered one might need five.

Particle effects in Unreal are built with Niagara. A single Niagara system is often several emitters working together: sparks, smoke and a heat shimmer from one cannon blast, or rain streaks, splashes and mist from one squall. Each emitter can use its own material.

Epic's issue tracker records a crash, UE-400816, that appears when you put those two facts together. With Substrate set to its blendable GBuffer mode, a Niagara system whose emitters use materials of different Substrate sizes can trip an assertion in the editor. Each effect is fine on its own. Together, they crash.

Where the seven comes from

When the renderer draws a primitive, it first asks, in effect, "what do I need to know about this thing for this view?" The answer is a bundle of flags called view relevance: is it opaque, is it translucent, does it cast a shadow, does it need velocity, and so on. When something is drawn from several materials, the engine merges their relevance into one bundle.

For yes or no flags, merging is easy. You combine them with a bitwise OR. If either material is translucent, the merged result is translucent. The engine's code even says so in a comment: the class only stores bits, so OR them all.

The Substrate slot count is not a yes or no flag. It is a small number packed into the same bundle. And numbers do not merge by OR.

Here is the arithmetic. Five in binary is 101. Two is 010. OR them and you get 111, which is seven. The right answer for "how many slots does the bigger of these two need" is five. The merge reports seven, the per pixel byte count is inflated, and under the blendable GBuffer an assertion fires.

What makes this one instructive is that the engine already knows the right answer. The base material relevance type in Unreal does handle this field correctly: it ORs everything, then puts the Substrate count back as the larger of the two values. The type that inherits from it, the primitive view relevance, has its own merge that walks the structure byte by byte and ORs everything, and it never repeats that correction. Two pieces of code, one careful and one not, sitting a few lines apart in the inheritance chain.

We did not take this on faith from a forum post. We opened the engine headers that ship with our installed 5.8 and read both merge functions. The careful one and the careless one are both there. The issue is real in the engine we build on.

There is a useful corollary in the math, too. If two emitters use materials with the same Substrate size, OR gives back that same number, and nothing goes wrong. Only different sizes can inflate. That tells an artist where to look the moment a multi emitter effect misbehaves.

Fixed, but not for us

Epic has fixed the bug. The tracker shows it resolved on 7 October, with the fix aimed at Unreal Engine 5.9.

That would normally be the end of the story. It is not, because in June Epic said publicly that 5.8 is the last planned major release of Unreal Engine 5. A 5.9 may still happen "if needed", and Unreal Engine 6 is targeted for Early Access at the end of 2027. So a fix marked for 5.9 is a fix for a release that may never come.

For a studio building a long lived online game on 5.8, that changes how we read every issue page. Our rule from today:

  • "Target 5.9" means "not coming to our engine" until Epic says otherwise. We plan around the bug instead of waiting for it.
  • Workarounds stay in place. For this one, the working rule for anyone building effects is to open new multi emitter Niagara systems in a scene alongside the effects they will share the screen with, rather than one material at a time, and to check the Substrate size of each emitter's material when something asserts.
  • We read the engine before we believe a report. A claim about the engine is a claim until someone has opened the file and found the line.

Two smaller lessons from the same week

Hotfixes are worth reading line by line. Epic shipped the 5.8.3 hotfix in late September. Its notes include fixes for procedural generation attribute drift, a streaming geometry assert, a level of detail commandlet running out of GPU address space, and Nanite level of detail choices under dynamic resolution. Several of those touch tools we lean on to build a world this size. Hotfixes also sometimes regress things, so we will take this one through our normal build process with a full rebuild and a module load check rather than clicking update on a busy day.

A memory graph can lie. A developer on Epic's forums reported that a 5.8 dedicated server, built in the Development configuration with the Iris replication system, slowly gains memory as actors spawn and die. Their reading is that the growth comes from debug bookkeeping (trace names and visual logger maps) rather than game code, and that it disappears when those features are compiled out. Epic has not confirmed it, so we treat it as a report. The lesson holds either way: when we soak test servers, the build configuration gets written down next to every memory number, and a rising floor in a debug build is a question to ask, not a verdict on our code.

Why we write these up

COGHEIM's cities walk across a living world, and the effects that sell that, steam off a hull, rain across a deck, sparks from a broadside, are exactly the kind of layered, multi emitter systems this bug lives in. We would rather find the seven in the source than in a crash report from a player.

If you build in Unreal 5.8 with Substrate, it is worth ten minutes to check your own effects. And if you have found a cleaner way to live with long tail engine bugs on a version that will not get another major release, we would like to hear it.
