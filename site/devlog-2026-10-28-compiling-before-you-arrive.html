Compiling Before You Arrive

Why PC games hitch the first time they draw something new, what this month's shader delivery news changes, and how we are preparing COGHEIM for it.

Published28 October 2026
CategoryEngine & Tooling
SeriesDev Brief

Header image: A lamp lit gallery hall inside one of COGHEIM's structures, with a caged lift and a grand stair, captured before a lighting pass. Engine capture, work in progress.

If you play on PC, you know the hitch. You step into a new room, or fire a weapon for the first time, or watch a storm roll in, and the game freezes for a fraction of a second. Do the same thing again and it is smooth. Most of the time, that first stutter is shader compilation.

This month brought real news on that front, so this post explains the problem, what changed, and what it means for a game like COGHEIM.

What a shader is, and why your PC compiles it late

Every surface you see in a modern game is drawn by small programs that run on the graphics card. These are shaders. A game ships them in an intermediate form, and before the card can run one, the graphics driver has to translate it into code for that exact card and that exact driver version.

On a console the hardware never changes, so this work happens once, at the studio, before the game ships. On PC there are thousands of combinations of cards and drivers, so the final step happens on your machine.

It gets harder. The card does not run a shader on its own. It runs a full pipeline: a set of shaders plus the settings for blending, depth, and output formats. Unreal calls these pipeline state objects, or PSOs. The engine often cannot know which exact pipeline it needs until something is about to be drawn. If drawing has to wait for that pipeline to compile, the frame waits too. That wait is the hitch.

How Unreal Engine handles it today

Unreal Engine 5 already has two tools for this.

The first is PSO precaching. When an object loads, the engine predicts which pipelines it will need and compiles them in the background, ideally before the object is ever on screen.

The second is a bundled pipeline cache. A studio plays its own game, records which pipelines were actually used, and ships that list so the game can compile them during startup or loading instead of during play.

Both help. Neither removes the problem. Precaching can miss, the recorded list is only as good as the play that produced it, and the first launch still has to do the compiling somewhere. We also read how the engine treats failure. A pipeline that fails while being precached is tolerated, because it was only a guess. A pipeline that is needed right now for drawing and fails to compile stops the program. That is a sensible rule, and it is also a reason to treat shader work as a real engineering budget rather than an afterthought.

What changed this month

Microsoft has opened up something called Advanced Shader Delivery. The idea is simple: instead of every player's machine compiling the same shaders, the store compiles them in advance for each supported family of graphics cards and drivers, and the player downloads the finished results along with the game. Microsoft says it is available now through its own store and the Xbox PC app, and that it will "soon" reach the Epic Games Store and Steam. Epic added the developer tools in Unreal Engine 5.8.4, and its documentation lists Epic Games Store support as planned for early 2027.

The developer side works like this: the studio builds the game, gathers a description of its shaders and recorded pipelines into a database, and submits that to the store. The store does the compiling.

For a large world full of materials, that is genuinely good news.

The fine print that matters to us

We read Epic's documentation closely, and five details shape what this means for COGHEIM.

Ray tracing pipelines are not covered. Epic's page says so plainly. COGHEIM is lit with Lumen running on hardware ray tracing, so part of our shader work will stay on the player's machine no matter which store they buy from.

Coverage depends on the recorded cache. Ordinary graphics pipelines enter the database through the same kind of recorded pipeline list described above. If a pipeline was never recorded, the store cannot compile it ahead of time.

Only some cards and drivers benefit. The store builds results for supported hardware and a limited set of recent drivers. Everyone else falls back to compiling locally.

The tool has to be built from engine source. It is not shipped as a ready made program, and the engine version we build on today predates it. That is a step we would take deliberately, not casually.

COGHEIM is a PC game first, and Steam has not announced a date. We are not going to plan around a service on the storefront that matters most to us until that storefront says when.

How COGHEIM is handling it

None of the following is finished or tested. It is the plan, and the order we intend to do it in.

Keep the engine's own protection on. PSO precaching is on by default in Unreal Engine 5.8, and we checked our project settings to confirm we have not turned it off.

Treat shader count as a design budget. COGHEIM is built from modular pieces, and city hulls carry liveries and weathering. Every combination of material, variant, and wear state can become another pipeline to compile. The answer is fewer master materials with more parameters, instead of a new material for every look. It costs nothing at the design stage and a great deal later.

Record from real play, not a test map. A walking city mixes interiors, exteriors, weather, and machinery in a single session. When we have cooked test builds, the pipeline cache we record should come from someone actually living aboard and travelling, because that is the session players will have.

Measure ray tracing separately. Since no store service will compile those pipelines for us, we will look for ray tracing hitches as their own line item.

Watch Steam, and do nothing early. When Valve announces support, the database step becomes worth building. Until then, the work above helps every player on every store.

Why we care this early

A hitch on first sight of something new is a small annoyance in most games. In COGHEIM it lands on the exact moments we want to matter most. The fantasy is a city that walks, carrying you into places you have not seen. The first time your city crests a ridge into weather you have never met, the engine is meeting new shaders at the same instant you are meeting a new horizon. We would rather that moment feel like arrival than like a stall.
