The Ceiling at 8192

A dense test forest ran out of room in Lumen's lighting cache, and the fix taught us where a settings line belongs.

Published11 November 2026
CategoryEngine & Tooling
SeriesDev Brief

Header image: A weeping willow and pines on a sunlit slope in one of our forest regions, captured in the editor. Work in progress.

Forests are hard on lighting. Every trunk, every hanging willow strand and every fern catches light, bounces it and shadows the ground under it. This week one of our forest regions taught us something about how Unreal Engine 5 keeps track of all that light, and about where a single line of configuration should live. The picture at the top of this post is from that region, captured in the editor. It is work in progress.

How Lumen remembers light

COGHEIM uses Lumen, the dynamic global illumination system in Unreal Engine 5, with hardware ray tracing. When Lumen traces a ray and it hits a surface, it needs to know how bright that surface is right now. It does not work that out from scratch for every ray. It looks the answer up in a cache.

That cache is called the surface cache. For each mesh in view, Lumen places a handful of flat "cards" around it, a little like photographers standing around a statue, and captures what the mesh looks like from each side: its colour, its normal, its depth, how much light it gives off. Those captures are packed into a set of large textures called the atlas, and Lumen lights the atlas over time. Rays that hit the world read from it.

The atlas has a fixed size. In a town square with a few dozen buildings, that is plenty. In a forest, it fills up fast, because every tree, rock and bush wants its own cards, and close to the camera each card wants more pixels.

The warning

While we were dressing a dense test forest, the engine started printing a message on screen in our test captures: the surface cache atlas was oversubscribed, at one point by about 72 percent, and it suggested raising the atlas size setting. When the atlas is oversubscribed, Lumen has to lower card resolution or drop cards, and lighting on some surfaces gets blurrier or slower to update.

The suggested fix is a single console variable, and the obvious move is to raise it. We did: from the engine's default of 4096 to 8192.

Then we read the engine source to find out what that number actually does, because a setting changed in a hurry is a setting nobody understands later.

Lesson one: 8192 is the ceiling

The atlas is built from square pages 128 texels on a side, and the engine allows at most 64 pages along each edge. Sixty four times 128 is 8192. Any value above that is clamped back down to the same atlas.

So the fix we reached for first is also the last time that particular lever will move. If a future forest is denser still, raising this number again will do nothing at all. The room has to come from somewhere else: fewer unique meshes that need their own cards, fewer cards per mesh, or cards at lower density where the eye will not notice.

The second thing the source told us is the cost. Every layer of the surface cache, from colour and normals through to the lit result, is allocated at the full atlas size. Going from 4096 to 8192 doubles the width and doubles the height, so each layer holds four times as many texels. That is graphics memory spent whether or not the scene in front of you needs it.

Lesson two: where a setting lives decides who can change it

This one matters more for players than for us.

Unreal Engine settings carry a priority that records who set them. The scalability system, which is what a Low, Medium, High or Epic graphics menu drives, sets values at a low priority. A value written into the project's main engine configuration under its console variables section is applied at a higher one. When something tries to change a setting at a lower priority than the one that set it, the engine refuses, and at normal log levels it does not say so.

Our first fix put 8192 in exactly that high priority section. In the editor that looked fine. In a shipped game, it would mean a player who lowers lighting quality to fit a smaller graphics card still pays for the largest possible surface cache, and nothing on screen would tell them why the setting did not help.

Nobody has played a build with this in it. We found it by reading the code path, which is the cheapest time to find something like this.

How we are handling it

  • Move the value to where it belongs. The larger atlas goes into the project's scalability configuration, only for the high and epic lighting levels. Lower levels keep Epic's defaults. Then we check the startup log to confirm which section applied it.
  • Measure, do not guess. The same forest view, at 1080p, with 4096 and with 8192, and the graphics memory difference written down beside it. A budget is a number, not a feeling.
  • Treat the forest as the budget, not the setting. Since the setting is at its ceiling, the next time the warning appears the answer is in the content: how many distinct tree and plant meshes a region uses, and how many cards each one asks for.
  • Watch what comes next. The Unreal Engine 5.9 Preview, released this week, adds a Lumen World Cache that builds its lighting cache from rays in world space and does not use cards at all. Epic's own page lists foliage among the features it does not support yet, so it is not a forest fix today. We are not moving to a Preview engine. We will read the full release when it ships.

Why this matters for a game like COGHEIM

COGHEIM is a game where the world is large, the cities walk across it, and players live inside them. A lot of what you will see is dense: forests, crowded decks, the inside of a moving city with a hundred lamps in it. Systems like Lumen are what make that look alive, and they are also where a game's memory quietly goes.

The habit we are building is simple. When the engine tells us to raise a number, we find out what the number does, where its limit is, and who is allowed to change it after we ship. This week that took one afternoon of reading. Finding it after launch, through players on smaller graphics cards wondering why the Low setting did not help, would have cost much more.
